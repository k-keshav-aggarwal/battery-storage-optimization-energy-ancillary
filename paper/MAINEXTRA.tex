\begin{abstract}

Battery energy storage systems (BESS) in peer-to-peer (P2P) electricity markets make most of their annual profits during rare, extreme price spikes.
However, existing market filters mistakenly treat genuine price spikes as corrupted data or cyberattacks, shutting down battery discharge and forfeiting up to a week of arbitrage revenue in a single hour.
This paper proposes an end-to-end framework that combines an unsupervised recurrent neural network (RNN) with reinforcement-learning-based dispatch and can be directly deployed in existing battery controllers. 
A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) autoencoder scans 24-hour rolling market prices to detect scarcity peaks; second, a tabular Q-learning controller uses this alert to hold stored energy and discharge at maximum profit.
Unlike state-of-the-art (SOTA) models that assume unrealistic advanced knowledge of tomorrow's prices, our system operates strictly causally. It introduces an economic break-even precision rule to protect against false alarms.
The system is evaluated on four years of authentic California Independent System Operator (CAISO) Day-Ahead settlement prices across three trading hubs (SP15, NP15, ZP26).
The GRU detector achieves 0.882 Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), enabling the Q-learning controller to quadruple trading profits while reducing cross-seed policy variance by 90\%. Against adversarial fake-spike attacks, our XGBoost defense achieves 97.4\% precision and recovers up to 71.6\% of attack damage, comfortably exceeding the break-even requirement (operating at 76.5\%), where static rules suffer severe losses.
This framework demonstrates that price spikes should be treated as exploitable trading signals rather than rejected as errors, providing an immediately deployable tool to boost battery revenue without altering grid-clearing rules.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction \& Literature Review}
\label{sec:intro}

Electricity prices in California have grown considerably more unpredictable in recent years. Solar output during the afternoon sun usually pushes locational marginal price (LMP)s down toward zero, and sometimes below it, only for prices to snap back once the sun sets and gas and coal plants jack up prices to cover the evening peak. Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) exploit this daily rhythm in decentralized power grids and peer-to-peer (P2P) trading frameworks to charge their systems when power is cheap and discharge as prices climb~\cite{Chen_DRL_BESS, P2P_Systems_Book}.

Most days, these curves follow a common pattern dictated by the work schedule and the weather. However, a battery's annual profits tend to depend on a handful of extreme hours. Hourly prices can soar over \$1,000/MWh during heatwaves, transmission outages, and sudden generator trips, and a 10 MW / 10 MWh battery that fully discharges just once during such a spike can profit about as much as it would have made in a whole week otherwise. It is therefore a high priority for storage operators to detect these rare hours as soon as possible and to anticipate them. 

Past state-of-the-art (SOTA) approaches to anomaly detection in pricing have addressed the problem primarily from a cybersecurity perspective~\cite{Vulnerability_Analysis, AE_Anomaly, Xie_FDI}. Such an approach starts by using historical data without anomalies, creates some artificial price spikes, and checks whether the added anomalies are detected. Such an approach may be useful in defending against a cyber-attack~\cite{Kurt_FDI_Detection}. However, it cannot prove the actual trading process, the origins of prices, or the legal procedures governing market settlements. If a legitimate \$1,000/MWh spike anomaly is detected and battery discharge is disabled, the operator will incur significant income loss.

Thus, to explain the reason for our experimental evaluations, we use only actual market prices, obtaining settlement information from the California Independent System Operator (CAISO) Open Access Same-Time Information System (OASIS) website~\cite{CAISO} without any synthetic additions. We also investigate common questions, such as: Does the attacker actually inject fake spikes into this data flow, and how can storage assets defend against such tampering?

Using historical market data has two crucial issues. The first is that the historical prices provided by market operators cannot be taken as ground-truth labels; therefore, the model needs to be validated for statistical consistency and, ultimately, profitability. The second and even more important issue is that mistakes are not symmetrical in any sense. While a mistake in image or text recognition is just an annoyance, a mistake that prevents the battery from discharging into a real \$1,000/MWh spike can lead to a loss.

This work sits at the intersection of electricity price forecasting~\cite{Lago_EPF_Benchmark}, unsupervised time-series anomaly detection~\cite{AE_Anomaly, Audibert_USAD}, generative modeling~\cite{VAE, DDPM}, P2P energy trading~\cite{Chen_DRL_BESS, P2P_Systems_Book}, and battery dispatch optimization~\cite{RL_Dispatch, Shi_BESS_Coopt, He_CAISO_BESS, Sutton_RL}.

Electricity Prices and Anomaly Detection:
Rolling statistics, moving averages, and local z-scores are traditional approaches for detecting abnormal price levels in SOTA models. Although these are one of the simplest approaches to apply, they have limitations: they assume constant thresholds that do not change with the seasons; a threshold set in winter to accommodate low load levels will always result in alarms in summer.

The deep models learn the usual 24-hour price pattern directly from historical data; there are no predefined patterns in our models. The recurrent autoencoders (Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)) transfer the sequence states forward through time and compress each daily path to a small-size bottleneck and detect the moments when the error of reconstruction becomes large~\cite{Audibert_USAD}. On the other hand, the variational autoencoders (VAE) encode price windows into a continuous latent space~\cite{VAE}, and the denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM) add Gaussian noise to the input and learn to remove this process~\cite{DDPM}. Large price spikes are unusual, making it harder to remove noise and leading to frequent, large errors.

Battery Dispatch and Trading: Storage scheduling, commonly used methods include model predictive control (MPC), linear programming, and dynamic programming~\cite{Shi_BESS_Coopt}, while reinforcement learning (RL) is increasingly seen as an alternative to these methods~\cite{RL_Dispatch, Chen_DRL_BESS, Sutton_RL}. One of the flaws frequently found in benchmarks reported in the literature is an implicit assumption of precognition about tomorrow's prices; when the optimizer is aware of the prices in advance, then there is no need for anomaly detection at all. This problem is avoided by ensuring that every dispatch instance is performed in a purely causal manner.

The reasons behind these two problems, lack of labeled data and imbalanced misclassification costs, inspire the design choices made in this paper. The paper has contributions in the following aspects:
\begin{itemize}
\item Benchmarking of seven different detector architectures—Isolation Forest (iForest), a VAE, a denoising diffusion model, single-node LSTM and GRU autoencoders, a multivariate LSTM, and a graph attention network—on 28,271 hours of authentic CAISO settlement data (expanding across four calendar years, 2023--2026, to 32,832 continuous market hours) across five random seeds and two disjoint test windows.
\item Dispatch policies spanning rules-based, directional, and RL methods to understand how controllers exploit anomaly signals, demonstrating that tabular Q-learning achieves maximum exploitation of the detection signal.
\item Economic break-even point precision analysis ($p^*$) assesses defense feasibility from an asymmetric-risk perspective under adversarial price feed poisoning attacks.
\end{itemize}

\label{sec:math_model}

Discrete-time state-of-charge (SOC) dynamics govern the physical operation of the BESS:
\begin{equation}
SOC_{t+1} = SOC_t + \left( \eta_{\text{ch}} P_t^{\text{ch}} - \frac{1}{\eta_{\text{dis}}} P_t^{\text{dis}} \right) \Delta t,
\label{eq:soc_dynamics}
\end{equation}

subject to operational power rating and storage capacity limits:
\begin{align}
0 \le P_t^{\text{ch}} \le P_{\max}, \quad 0 \le P_t^{\text{dis}} \le P_{\max}, \label{eq:p_limits} \\
0 \le SOC_t \le C, \label{eq:soc_limits}
\end{align}
where $P_t^{\text{ch}}$ and $P_t^{\text{dis}}$ denote power at hour $t$, respectively, with $P_{\max} = 10$\,MW, $C = 10$\,MWh, and $\Delta t = 1$\,h. Symmetric one-way conversion efficiencies satisfy $\eta_{\text{ch}} = \eta_{\text{dis}} = \sqrt{e} = \sqrt{0.80} \approx 0.894$, guaranteeing a round-trip cycle efficiency of $e = \eta_{\text{ch}}\eta_{\text{dis}} = 0.80$.

The deep sequential model considers normalized 24-hour sliding price vectors and evaluates their anomaly score by means of mean squared reconstruction error:

\begin{equation}
s_t = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left(x_{t,h}-\hat{x}_{t,h}\right)^2.
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Usually, the autoencoder successfully reconstructs the price graph for the particular day. However, when prices increase suddenly, the compressed representation cannot encode the change, and $s_t$ increases sharply.

In the GNN-AE model, we use the graph representation $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ for the three regional hubs, where $\mathcal{V} = \{\text{NP15}, \text{ZP26}, \text{SP15}\}$, and the edge set $\mathcal{E}$ contains the main transmission connections for Path 15 (between NP15 and ZP26) and Path 26 (between ZP26 and SP15). In the graph attention network, two graph attention layers are used (hidden units of 8), and the directional spatial attention weight $\alpha_{ij}$ is calculated by the following equations:

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(\text{LeakyReLU}\left(\mathbf{a}^T [\mathbf{W} h_i \parallel \mathbf{W} h_j]\right)\right)}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i} \exp\left(\text{LeakyReLU}\left(\mathbf{a}^T [\mathbf{W} h_i \parallel \mathbf{W} h_k]\right)\right)},
    \label{eq:gat}
    \end{equation}

where $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{8 \times d}$ is a shared projection matrix, $\mathbf{a}$ is the attention vector, $\parallel$ is concatenation, and $\mathcal{N}_i$ denotes neighboring regional hubs. Spatial node representations are followed by LSTM-based temporal sequence encoding.


Tabular Q-learning~\cite{Sutton_RL} learns to dispatch in a discrete state space defined by $s=(p_z, a_{\text{flag}}, s_{\text{soc}})$. Discrete z-normalized price is $N_p=10$ bins in the range from $-3$ to $+3$; anomaly flag is $N_a=5$ categories (or $N_a=1$ for unconditional version); battery SOC ratio ($SOC_t/C$) is $N_s=5$ bins resulting in $|S|=250$ discrete states ($|S|=50$ without anomaly flag). The action space contains three discrete power actions $a \in \{-1.0, 0.0, 1.0\}$ corresponding to full discharge ($P_t^{\text{dis}}=P_{\max}$), idle ($P_t=0$), and full charge ($P_t^{\text{ch}}=P_{\max}$), subject to the physical constraints in \eqref{eq:soc_dynamics}--\eqref{eq:soc_limits}. The net cash return reward $r_t$ received by the agent accounts for market revenue, degradation, and fees~\cite{Xu_BESS_Degradation}:
\begin{align}
    r_t ={}& \left( p_t P_t^{\text{dis}} - p_t P_t^{\text{ch}} \right) \Delta t \nonumber \\
    &- \left[ c_{\text{fee}} \left( P_t^{\text{dis}} + P_t^{\text{ch}} \right) + c_{\text{deg}} P_t^{\text{dis}} \right] \Delta t.
    \label{eq:reward}
    \end{align}
    The action values are iteratively learned using Eq.~\eqref{eq:qlearning}:
    \begin{equation}
    Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha \left[r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)\right],
    \label{eq:qlearning}
    \end{equation}
with learning rate $\alpha=0.15$ and discount factor $\gamma=0.95$.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

We examine nearly four years of hourly day-ahead LMPs from CAISO spanning January 12, 2023, to October 11, 2026. We collected price data from the three major trading hubs, SP15, NP15, and ZP26, and four ancillary services (Regulation Up, Regulation Down, Spinning Reserves, and Non-Spinning Reserves) for a total of 28,271 raw hourly settlement observations (expanding to 32,832 continuous market hours under regular chronological grid indexing). The additional price streams serve as auxiliary contextual information for multi-channel anomaly detection, reflecting the overall tightness of operating reserves. At the same time, the economic trading calculations only evaluate day-ahead energy arbitrage. 

Price anomalies were registered during the above-mentioned time frame, with SP15 reaching its highest point of $\$1,143.78/\text{MWh}$ on August 17, 2023, due to heatwaves in the region, and its lowest point of $-\$41.33/\text{MWh}$ on May 19, 2024, due to excessive solar generation.

For a more realistic evaluation of our algorithm's effectiveness, we break down the time period into periods without any overlap among them: the training period between January 12, 2023 and December 31, 2024 (17,280 continuous hours, approximately 23.7 months), validation period between January 1 and June 30, 2025 (4,344 hours, 5.9 months: used only for threshold calibration), and testing period between July 1, 2025 and October 11, 2026 (11,208 hours, 15.3 months, evaluated once). We additionally evaluate a six-month out-of-sample seasonal test period between January 1 and June 30, 2025. In California wholesale markets, Day-Ahead Market (DAM) clearing occurs for all 24 hours of the subsequent operating day at 13:00 $D-1$. Nevertheless, BESS commercial traders and transactive prosumers must place their bids for energy deliveries without knowing the settled prices before 10:00 AM. They must roll their exposures to real-time imbalances on a 15-minute and 5-minute basis. We test our models using only causal 24-hour rolling windows in order to eliminate the problem of foreseeing.

Since it is not possible to use ground-truth anomaly flags in past settlement prices, a flag is made available for ex-post evaluation only for reference. The flag is considered an hour with a flag when the price is more than 4 median absolute deviations (MAD) from the 30-day moving median or within the top/bottom 0.5\% of historical prices (which occurs 739 times in 28,271 hours, or around 2.6\% of the time). None of the models is provided access to check the flag during the entire training process. Sensitivity analysis reveals that rankings of model families based on their performances are invariant with respect to the thresholds (3.0 to 5.0 MAD).

The detection pipeline (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}) uses a two-level filtering approach: iForest is used to isolate and remove major outliers, followed by analysis of the remaining possible hours using a deep-sequence model.

The Stage 1 iForest~\cite{IForest} considers nine historical-only causal features: (i) trailing 6-hour linear price slope $\Delta p / \Delta t$, (ii) non-reversion ratio $|p_t - p_{t-1}| / (|p_{t-1} - p_{t-2}| + \epsilon)$, (iii) 1-hour price difference $(p_t - p_{t-1})$, (iv) 3-hour price difference $(p_t - p_{t-3})$, (v) 24-hour trailing robust $z$-score $(p_t - \text{median}_{24}) / \text{MAD}_{24}$, (vi) 168-hour trailing $z$-score, (vii) 24-hour rolling volatility $\sigma_{24}$, and (viii--ix) cyclic diurnal harmonics $(\sin(2\pi h / 24), \cos(2\pi h / 24))$ for hour-of-day $h \in \{0, \dots, 23\}$.

A total of three models have been implemented for Stage 2 single-node detection. VAE has been trained for 40 epochs with the Adam optimizer ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$, batch size=64). The VAE architecture consists of fully connected networks with 48 neurons, and the bottleneck is modeled as an 8-dimensional Gaussian distribution~\cite{VAE}. LSTM-AE and GRU-AE map the price sequence through 32 recurrent neurons to an 8-dimensional bottleneck.

The neural network is trained using the Adam optimization algorithm, with a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, a batch size of 32, and 40 training epochs. The training uses the mean squared error loss function~\cite{Audibert_USAD}. In the GRU case, the input and forget gates are used, which makes training 25\% faster but does not affect temporal dependencies. The DDPM learns how to reverse the procedure of adding noise to the data on discrete schedules~\cite{DDPM}. The inference step involves corrupting the window samples at four noise levels ($t \in \{0.10, 0.25, 0.40, 0.60\}$) over 16 iterations and averaging the residuals.

To explore the influence of the regional transmission environment on predictability, we also feed SP15, NP15, and ZP26 as inputs to two multi-hub models, which include a 3-channel multivariate LSTM (MV-LSTM) control and the graph neural network autoencoder (GNN-AE)~\cite{GAT}.

All experiments were conducted with five random seeds (0-4), ensuring that \texttt{torch.set\_num\_threads(1)} was always set and that cuDNN flags were fixed to maintain determinism.
To evaluate the performance of the proposed framework, all evaluations were conducted on a commodity laptop host, a Dell Inspiron 15. The hardware execution layer features an Intel Core i7-1355U processor (10 cores, 1.70 GHz base clock, up to 5.0 GHz boost configuration), 16 GB of system RAM, and integrated Intel Iris Xe Graphics (128 MB). 
High-throughput dataset I/O operations were sustained with a 512 GB NVMe SSD running in a Microsoft Windows 11 environment. 
The core computational pipelines were implemented within a unified Python ecosystem utilizing numpy (v2.0+) for matrix and tensor computations, pandas (v2.2+) for settlement series indexing, and scipy (v1.14+) for linear programming via the HiGHS solver. 
Machine learning implementations and defensive modeling relied on scikit-learn (v1.5+) for iForest and validation metrics, and on torch (v2.4+) for deep autoencoders and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). Finally, gradient-boosted defenses were deployed using xgboost (v2.0+), and all performance and data visualizations were generated using matplotlib (v3.8+).

\section{Results and Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}

Thresholds for the budget percentile range from 0.5\% to 10\% were optimized based on the F1 score on the validation dataset and then fixed before evaluating the model on the test dataset.

The results for the quality of reconstruction and detection of anomalies by all methods are provided in Table~\ref{tab:detector_results}, measured in terms of root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). The ROC curves are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}, while Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline} shows detection results compared with the SP15 market price.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{test_timeline.png}

The GRU-AE (downward triangle), LSTM-AE (diamond), Diffusion (upward triangle), iForest (circle), and VAE (square) show their detection results compared with the SP15 market price. The inset shows the operational low-false-positive region (false positive rate $\text{FPR} \le 0.12$).}

Perhaps the most obvious trend from Table \ref{tab:detector_results} is that low reconstruction error does not necessarily indicate high-quality anomaly detection. While the VAE yielded the highest-quality reconstructions out of all single-node models (RMSE \$7.85/MWh), it generated the lowest detection scores (AUROC = 0.534; $F_1$ = 0.027). The VAE relies on the latent prior to generate Gaussian noise, so the output becomes smoothed, eliminating the exact signals needed for anomaly detection.

This is because recurrent autoencoders exhibited far better performance since they could handle temporal dependencies effectively. The AUROC values for the LSTM-AE and GRU-AE models were 0.859 and 0.882, respectively. As for the reason behind their superiority in this matter, one should note that, due to the sequential updating process, recurrent models maintain their memory of a typical daily pattern. Thus, upon encountering a sudden price change, the hidden state will be unable to reconcile the jump, and the error rate will skyrocket.

Incorporating price information from the three California centers increased the GNN-AE's mean AUROC to 0.938. However, the simple multivariate LSTM baseline performed nearly as well, achieving a mean AUROC of 0.854 and reaching 0.988 AUROC on three of the five seeds. Comparison of the two with a paired bootstrap yielded a 95\% confidence interval of $[-0.102, +0.281]$; as the interval includes zero, there is no statistically significant difference between using graph attention and using multivariate modeling alone. The added value seems to be mostly due to tracking price behavior in neighboring regions, rather than to graph attention.

The second test window for next year would demonstrate that the recurrent models can generalize across seasons, as the LSTM-AE achieved an AUROC of 0.921. On the other hand, the performance of the static iForest was extremely poor, as it could only get an AUROC of 0.453, worse than guessing randomly, because it is incapable of adapting to wholesale price variations from winter demand to summer solar/hydropower production.

The results obtained for a 5-channel diffusion model trained across all three hubs and two ancillary streams were an average AUROC of $0.528\pm0.066$, which is lower than the default at one hub ($0.564\pm0.071$). It seems like the additional price channels did nothing but add noise to the forward diffusion process, providing nothing to denoise on the reverse pass.

Turning Detection into Profit: To examine whether the anomaly alerts yield profits, we conducted trading simulations for a battery with a capacity of 10\,MWh/10\,MW ($e=0.80$, transaction cost \$0.50/MWh, degradation cost \$2.50/MWh~\cite{Xu_BESS_Degradation}).

Strategy 1 (Heuristic dispatch) is activated every time prices are less than the 25th percentile of the historical value and ends as soon as the prices become greater than the 75th percentile, with 5\, MW dispatch and 10\, MW dispatch as the event occurs. Strategy 2 (Directional spread) goes long or short 1\, MWh for every anomaly detected, with a 24-hour trading period. Strategy 3 (Tabular Q-learning)~\cite{Sutton_RL} learns to dispatch in the discrete state space $s=(p_z, a_{\text{flag}}, s_{\text{soc}})$ under an $\epsilon$-greedy exploration schedule decaying linearly from $\epsilon_{\text{start}}=0.30$ to $\epsilon_{\text{end}}=0.02$ across 30 training runs.

\subsection{Break-Even Precision Analysis ($p^*$)}

In the battery dispatch problem, a true positive (TP) identifies a fake spike, thus earning a profit ($v_{\text{TP}} > 0$). In contrast, a false positive (FP) misidentifies a genuine price spike, resulting in revenue loss ($v_{\text{FP}} < 0$). An operational defense that operates at a precision level of $p$ is said to be at the break-even point if the expected value of the cash flows:

\begin{equation}
    p^* = \frac{v_{\text{FP}}}{v_{\text{FP}} - v_{\text{TP}}} = \frac{|v_{\text{FP}}|}{|v_{\text{FP}}| + v_{\text{TP}}}.
    \label{eq:breakeven}
    \end{equation}

Because high absolute values trigger the rule indiscriminately, it forfeits the year's most lucrative genuine price spikes; hence, its break-even point rises to $p^* = 0.928$, demanding an unrealistically stringent 92.8\% precision threshold to avoid net capital depletion.

\section{Conclusion \& Future Directions}
\label{sec:discussion}

This paper investigated the challenge of detecting rare, high-value wholesale electricity price anomalies to enable profitable, tamper-resilient battery storage arbitrage in decentralized P2P transactive energy markets.

To address the asymmetric risk of false-alarm penalties without assuming unrealistic future price precognition, we developed an end-to-end causal framework coupling an unsupervised deep recurrent autoencoder with a tabular Q-learning dispatch controller. The architecture leverages dynamic reconstruction error signals to actively modulate prosumer storage actions under strictly causal 24-hour rolling market settlement feeds.

Evaluated across four calendar years  of authentic CAISO Day-Ahead settlement data, the GRU-AE achieved 0.882 AUROC, outperforming static and generative baselines by retaining temporal memory of normal diurnal rhythms. 
Conditioning tabular Q-learning on this anomaly alert quadrupled energy trading profits while reducing cross-seed policy variance by 90\%.
The learned adversarial defense comfortably satisfied the break-even precision requirement (operating at 76.5\%), where conventional threshold rules incurred punitive false-alarm losses.

The primary scientific insight of this work is that wholesale market price spikes should be exploited as high-value dispatch signals rather than dismissed as telemetry corruption, demonstrating that risk-aware anomaly conditioning resolves the fundamental trade-off between cyber defense and market profitability.

The proposed framework is directly deployable within modern battery management systems and transactive prosumer architectures without requiring regulatory restructuring or modifications to existing wholesale power market clearing rules.
Future extensions will evaluate the controller across sub-hourly market intervals and incorporate non-linear cycle-depth rainflow counting with cell thermal dynamics to optimize multi-year asset lifespans.
