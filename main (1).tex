\begin{abstract}
Battery energy storage systems (BESS) participating in energy markets face growing exposure to adversarial price manipulation, where artificial price spikes are injected into market signals to induce suboptimal charging and discharging decisions. Existing strategies either ignore such price events entirely, leaving the system fully exposed to manipulation, or apply blanket penalties that forfeit legitimate trading opportunities during genuine market stress. This paper proposes a two-stage framework combining a Variational Autoencoder (VAE)~\cite{VAE} with an ensemble of Isolation Forest (iForest)~\cite{IForest} and Local Outlier Factor (LOF)~\cite{LOF} detectors to classify hourly price deviations as either genuine market events or synthetic injections. The resulting anomaly signal is integrated under three operating regimes: baseline (anomaly-agnostic), penalized (anomaly-averse), and opportunistic (anomaly-adaptive). The genuine-versus-synthetic classifier achieves an $F_1$ score of $0.5859$ and a precision of $0.9615$ on 727 anomalous hours evaluated against honest ground truth derived from injected spike positions. Experiments on three years of California electricity price data show that the opportunistic regime achieves a $0.53\%$ profit improvement over the anomaly-agnostic baseline under adversarial conditions while maintaining comparable clean-market performance. A robustness sweep across 15 spike magnitude and duration configurations confirms consistent detection rates, scaling from $10.7\%$ at $1.5\times$ magnitude to $71.0\%$ at $8\times$ magnitude. Explainability of dispatch decisions is further supported by adapting Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)~\cite{GradCAM} to the temporal VAE setting, giving market operators a human-readable audit trail for every charge and discharge decision.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Battery energy storage systems (BESS) are becoming central to peer-to-peer (P2P) energy markets, where they provide price arbitrage, frequency regulation, and spinning reserve. Most of these systems now run on automated dispatch algorithms that respond directly to real-time price signals, and that dependence is exactly what makes them worth attacking. An adversary who can inject artificial price spikes into the market feed can push a battery into charging or discharging at the wrong moment, cutting into prosumer revenue and, at scale, putting real strain on grid stability~\cite{Vulnerability_Analysis}.

Southern California pricing nodes make a good test bed for this problem. The hub is one of the more actively traded parts of the CAISO market, and it already carries plenty of volatility from renewable variability and strategic bidding. Hence, an adversarial spike has to compete with substantial legitimate noise. Current dispatch strategies do not handle that noise well. One ignores anomalies altogether and treats every price tick as trustworthy, which leaves it fully exposed to manipulation. The other overcorrects: any statistically unusual price gets penalized or avoided outright, regardless of whether it came from an attacker or from a heatwave. That second strategy is safer against manipulation, but it also gives up real revenue during the exact periods, genuine supply shortfalls or demand surges, when a battery could be earning the most.

Deep generative models offer a way out of this trade-off, at least on the detection side. A VAE~\cite{VAE} trained on normal price behavior learns a compact latent representation of what "typical" 24-hour patterns look like, so reconstruction error becomes a natural anomaly signal when something deviates from that pattern. Classical density-based detectors such as iForest~\cite{IForest} and LOF~\cite{LOF} add a complementary view, since they are sensitive to different kinds of irregularity than a sequence model is. Even so, detection by itself does not tell you what to do about it. A spike that a detector flags as anomalous could be a real market event worth capturing, or it could be a synthetic injection worth ignoring, and a bare anomaly score has no way of distinguishing the two. That is the gap this paper tries to close: a classification layer that sits between detection and dispatch, judging each flagged spike on persistence, mean reversion, and rate of change before deciding whether the optimizer should lean into it or away from it.

\section{Related Work}

This work sits at the intersection of three fairly separate literatures: electricity price forecasting~\cite{Price_Forecasting}, anomaly detection in energy systems~\cite{AE_Anomaly}, and adversarial robustness in BESS dispatch~\cite{BESS}. Each has grown on its own, and none, on its own, answers the question this paper is actually interested in: whether a flagged anomaly should change what a battery does next, and by how much.

Statistical process control was the natural starting point for price anomaly detection: flag anything that exceeds some multiple of a rolling mean or standard deviation and call it a day. It is cheap, and for a while, that was enough. The problem is that a threshold rule has no memory of context. A demand spike during a heatwave and a five-minute synthetic injection can produce the same single-hour deviation, so a method that only looks at magnitude cannot separate them. ARIMA-family models improved on this somewhat, since they model the expected price as a function of its own history and use the residual, the gap between what was predicted and what happened, as the anomaly signal. That gives you more context than a fixed threshold, but the underlying assumption of linear, stationary dynamics does not hold up well during the regime shifts that real markets go through.

Deep learning changed the picture. LSTM-based forecasters brought reconstruction error into the anomaly-detection toolkit and turned out to be considerably better at catching multi-hour deviations than the statistical methods that came before them~\cite{LSTM_Anomaly}. VAEs~\cite{VAE} pushed this further still: because the latent space is probabilistic rather than deterministic, the model gives you a principled sense of how far an observation sits from what it considers normal, not just a raw reconstruction distance. What is missing from this line of work, though, is any attempt to close the loop. The VAE papers we are aware of stop at detection. None of them asks what a dispatch algorithm should actually do once a spike has been flagged, which is precisely the question this paper takes up.

Dispatch optimization has its own, largely separate history. Model predictive control treats dispatch as a rolling program that updates as new price forecasts come in~\cite{MPC_BESS}. Stochastic programming handles forecast uncertainty by optimizing over scenario trees, though at a real computational cost~\cite{BESS}. Reinforcement learning has also been used to learn dispatch policies directly from market interactions~\cite {RL_Dispatch, Vulnerability_Analysis}. However, several of these studies report that a well-tuned MPC baseline still beats the learned policy in volatile conditions, suggesting how hard this problem is to learn end-to-end.

What almost all of this work has in common, regardless of where it comes from, is an implicit assumption that the price feed itself can be trusted. Adversarial injection is not part of the picture, and there is no mechanism in this literature for treating an anomalous price differently depending on its source. That is the specific gap we are targeting: we extend the standard linear programming dispatch formulation with an anomaly-conditioned objective and constraint set, without giving up the tractability and auditability that make LP-based dispatch the practical choice for a regulated market in the first place.

\section{System Architecture and Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Overview}
The proposed framework processes raw electricity price data through four stages: data ingestion, anomaly detection, spike classification, and adaptive dispatch, producing a profit-maximizing charge-and-discharge schedule. Each stage is designed to be independent of the others: the anomaly detector can be swapped without modifying the optimizer, and the classification thresholds can be recalibrated for different market nodes without retraining the VAE. This modularity is deliberate, since operational energy systems require components that can be audited, updated, and replaced independently as market conditions or regulatory requirements change.

\subsection{Stage 1 - Data Ingestion and Preprocessing}
The pipeline ingests hourly locational marginal prices from the SP15, NP15, and ZP26 nodes of the CAISO market~\cite{CAISO}, along with four ancillary service markets: regulation up (RegUp), regulation down (RegDown), spinning reserve (Spin), and non-spinning reserve (NonSpin). To avoid repeated API calls across runs, all fetched data is stored immediately in a compressed CSV cache on first retrieval. Every subsequent run loads from this cache, which automatically restores date and time types and reduces execution time. A single aggregation step collapses the multi-node structure into a single hourly time series before passing the data downstream.

\subsection{Stage 2 - Ensemble Anomaly Detection}
The anomaly detector is a three-member ensemble, with each member contributing a distinct perspective on whether an hourly price observation is normal or anomalous. The VAE~\cite{VAE}, the primary member, is trained on the first 80\% of the time series using 24-hour input sequences. At inference time, the hours the VAE struggles to reconstruct incur a high per-timestep reconstruction error, which forms the VAE's continuous anomaly score. The secondary members, iForest~\cite{IForest} and LOF~\cite{LOF}, are classical unsupervised outlier detectors that approach the problem from a density perspective, using continuous \texttt{score\_samples()} rather than binary classification: prices in sparsely populated regions of the distribution receive higher anomaly scores regardless of temporal context. All three detectors are trained exclusively on the training split, and the held-out test set is never used during fitting, preventing information leakage. The three continuous scores are combined into a single ensemble score in $[0, 1]$ through weighted summation, with weights of $0.5$, $0.3$, and $0.2$ respectively.

\subsection{Stage 3 - Genuine vs.\ Synthetic Spike Classification}
A high anomaly score alone is not enough to determine how the dispatch optimizer should respond: a genuine demand surge caused by a heatwave and a synthetic price injection can both produce high scores, but the correct dispatch response to each is the opposite. The classification stage resolves this ambiguity by combining three behavioral signals into a continuous $is\_genuine$ score in $[0, 1]$. First, a persistence signal checks whether the elevated price holds across a rolling window, since genuine market stress events typically sustain elevated prices for several consecutive hours. Second, a non-reversion signal measures how far the current price exceeds the forward rolling mean: genuine shocks stay elevated relative to their near-future mean, while synthetic injections revert sharply and fall below that mean quickly. Third, a rate-of-change signal captures near-instantaneous step jumps, a common signature of synthetic data injection but rare in real markets, where price formation involves continuous participant bidding. Rather than a hard binary label, these three signals are combined with weights of $0.4$, $0.3$, and $0.3$, respectively, into the continuous $is\_genuine$ score, which the optimizer treats as a soft weight rather than an on-or-off switch. This probabilistic design allows the optimizer to partially hedge when classification confidence is low.

\subsection{Stage 4 - Anomaly-Adaptive Dispatch Optimization}
The dispatch optimizer is a linear program solved using the HiGHS solver~\cite{HiGHS} via Pyomo~\cite{Pyomo}. It selects an hourly charge and discharge schedule to maximize total operating profit, subject to the battery's physical constraints: SoC evolves according to round-trip efficiency, discharge is bounded by available stored energy, and charge is bounded by remaining capacity. Transaction fees and per-cycle degradation costs are included in the objective to reflect real operational economics. The three operating regimes differ only in how the anomaly signal modulates the sell constraint and objective coefficients. In the baseline regime, anomaly scores are ignored entirely. In the penalized regime, sell limits and effective price weights are reduced in proportion to the anomaly score, so the battery trades less aggressively whenever an anomaly is detected. In the opportunistic regime, the proposed method decomposes the anomaly score using the $is\_genuine$ weight: confirmed genuine spikes raise the sell limit and increase the effective sell price. In contrast, suspected synthetic spikes lower the sell limit. The degree of response in both directions is governed by the sensitivity parameter $\lambda \in [0, 1]$.

\subsection{Explainability via Temporal Grad-CAM}

To address the interpretability requirements that accompany any AI-assisted trading system operating in a regulated market, the framework adapts Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)~\cite{GradCAM} to the temporal VAE setting. In its original image-classification setting, Grad-CAM computes the gradient of the target class score with respect to the final feature map, revealing which spatial regions of the input drove the model's prediction. We apply the same idea along the time axis rather than the spatial axis: taking the gradient of the VAE's total reconstruction error with respect to each hour in the 24-hour input sequence tells us which hours the model found most anomalous and, therefore, most influential in shaping the dispatch signal.

The resulting per-hour influence scores are aligned with the original hourly time axis and stored alongside the dispatch output, giving a market operator a human-readable explanation of why the system chose to charge, discharge, or remain idle at any given hour. \section{Model Equations and Parameters}

The battery SoC is updated according to

\begin{equation}
SOC_t = SOC_{t-1} + \left( buy_t \cdot e - \frac{sell_t}{e} \right) dt
\end{equation}

where charging and discharging are constrained by

\begin{equation}
sell_t \leq e \cdot SOC_{t-1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
buy_t \cdot e \leq Capacity_{\mathrm{MWh}} - SOC_{t-1}
\end{equation}

The maximum discharge power is limited by

\begin{equation}
sell_t \leq P_{\max}
\end{equation}

The objective is to maximize net revenue:

\begin{equation}
\max \sum_t \Bigl( sell_t \cdot price_t - buy_t \cdot price_t - cost \cdot (sell_t + buy_t) \Bigr)
\end{equation}

\section{Dataset Description}

\subsection{Data Sources}
The dataset was obtained from the California Independent System Operator (CAISO) wholesale electricity market~\cite{CAISO} via the \texttt{gridstatus} API. Three major trading hubs were selected to represent geographically diverse regions of the California power system:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SP15} (South Path 15 Trading Hub)
    \item \textbf{NP15} (North Path 15 Trading Hub)
    \item \textbf{ZP26} (Valley Electric Trading Hub)
\end{itemize}

Ancillary service market prices were also retrieved to provide operational signals: Regulation Up (RegUp), Regulation Down (RegDown), Spinning Reserve (Spin), and Non-Spinning Reserve (NonSpin).

\subsection{Data Collection Procedure}
Historical market data spanning January 2023 through December 2025 were obtained through the CAISO day-ahead market interface. To improve reliability and avoid API timeouts, the data acquisition process used chunk-based retrieval, dividing the full time range into 15-day intervals. Failed requests were automatically retried before being discarded. Retrieved datasets were cached locally to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Dataset Characteristics}
The final dataset consists of 77,832 hourly market observations across three trading hubs. Each record contains the Locational Marginal Price (LMP) value and ancillary service prices aligned to a common timestamp.

\subsection{Synthetic Anomaly Generation}
To test detection performance under adversarial conditions, we built a synthetic attack dataset by injecting controlled price spikes, using a local random number generator with a fixed seed 
to ensure reproducibility. For each injected event, we picked a random temporal window and scaled the original LMP signal within it by a configurable magnitude and duration. Ninety percent of injected events were non-reverting spikes, meant to simulate sustained adversarial manipulation rather than a one-off blip. We also kept a Boolean spike mask indicating which hours were injected so that the classifier could be evaluated against honest ground truth.

Two datasets were maintained throughout all experiments:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clean Dataset}: Original CAISO market data.
    \item \textbf{Attack Dataset}: Market data with 16 synthetic price spike events (88 injected hours).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Train/Test Partitioning}
We partitioned the dataset chronologically rather than randomly to preserve temporal dependencies and avoid leaking future information into the training set. All three detectors, VAE, iForest, and LOF, were fit exclusively on the training split (80\%), and the held-out test set was used only for final evaluation.

\subsection{Dataset Challenges}
Several characteristics of electricity market data present challenges for anomaly detection:

\begin{itemize}
    \item High price volatility and rare extreme spikes.
    \item Severe class imbalance between normal and anomalous events.
    \item Regional market differences between trading hubs.
    \item Seasonal and operational regime changes over multi-year spans.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{VAE Reconstruction Performance}
The VAE was trained on the first 80\% of each scenario's time series and evaluated on the remaining 20\%. The VAE is not designed as a conventional price forecaster; it is trained as a density model that learns the distribution of normal 24-hour price sequences. Under this framing, $R^2$ is not the primary evaluation metric here: a model that achieves high $R^2$ by tracking prices closely, including anomalous ones, would defeat the purpose of anomaly detection. The clean scenario yields an $R^2$ of $0.0625$ and the attack scenario $0.1251$, both near zero and consistent with a model that generalizes to typical patterns without overfitting to extremes. Reconstruction accuracy improves from $21.57\%$ under clean conditions to $24.17\%$ under attack, a $12.06\%$ relative increase, confirming that the VAE's reconstruction error grows in the presence of injected anomalies, precisely the signal the downstream stages rely on. Full metrics are in Table~\ref{tab:performance_comparison}, and Fig.~\ref{fig:output} shows a representative 7-day reconstruction trace.

\subsection{Anomaly Detection and Classification Performance}
The anomaly detection pipeline blends per-timestep VAE reconstruction error, continuous iForest outlierness scores, and continuous LOF scores into a unified ensemble anomaly score with weights $0.5$, $0.3$, and $0.2$, respectively. A behavioral classification stage then assigns each flagged hour a continuous $is\_genuine$ score.

To evaluate the genuine-versus-synthetic classifier, the $is\_genuine$ score was assessed against 727 anomalous hours (ensemble score $> 0.1$) using the honest spike-mask ground truth: hours directly modified by injection are labeled synthetic (0), and all other hours are labeled genuine (1). Of the 727 evaluated hours, 15 correspond to injected synthetic spikes, and 712 are genuine market events. The classifier achieves a precision of $0.9615$, a recall of $0.4213$, and an $F_1$ score of $0.5859$ for the genuine class, with an AUROC of $0.2228$. Classifier metrics are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:classifier}.

The low synthetic recall reflects both the severe class imbalance (15 synthetic vs.\ 712 genuine anomalous hours) and the difficulty of the classification problem: synthetic spikes designed to mimic genuine market behavior share many of the same persistence and rate-of-change features as real events. Calibrating the $is\_genuine$ threshold using the Youden-J criterion~\cite{Youden} is a clear direction for improving synthetic recall in future work.

\subsection{BESS Dispatch Optimization Results}
BESS dispatch is solved as a linear program using HiGHS~\cite{HiGHS} via Pyomo~\cite{Pyomo}. Three strategies are evaluated: Baseline, Penalized, and Opportunistic. Under the standard evaluation with $\lambda = 0.3$, the opportunistic regime achieves a profit of \$487.7\, K under clean conditions and \$493.4\, K under attack, compared to the baseline's \$485.1\, K and \$490.8\, K, a $0.53\%$ improvement in both scenarios. Strategy profits and EFC statistics are summarized in Tables~\ref{tab:strategy_results} and~\ref{tab:efc_profit_cycle}.

The opportunistic regime consistently outperforms both baseline and penalized strategies across the full $\lambda$ range (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}). The penalized strategy converges to a stable lower bound as $\lambda$ approaches $1.0$, reflecting its increasing reluctance to trade during anomalous periods. \subsection{Comparison Against SOTA Methods}
The proposed VAE ensemble was compared against two simpler anomaly detectors within the same opportunistic dispatch pipeline: a rolling z-score threshold and a standalone iForest using continuous outlierness scores. Under attack conditions, the z-score threshold achieves a profit change of $-0.98\%$ over baseline, the standalone iForest achieves $+0.27\%$, and the proposed VAE ensemble achieves $+0.53\%$. These results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:sota}.

The VAE ensemble is the only detector that consistently improves over the baseline in both clean and attack scenarios. The z-score threshold underperforms because it flags legitimate volatility periods as anomalous, causing the optimizer to suppress trades during genuine price events. The iForest alone captures point-level outliers but lacks the temporal coherence of the VAE~\cite{VAE}, leading to noisier anomaly signals. The VAE ensemble's advantage comes from combining temporal structure (VAE), density-level sensitivity (iForest~\cite{IForest}), and local neighborhood context (LOF~\cite{LOF}) into a more reliable $is\_genuine$ score.

\section{Limitations}

The profit gains reported above are real, but they come with limitations worth being upfront about.

The most important one is that the genuine-versus-synthetic classifier is not actually catching synthetic spikes. Within the 727 anomalous hours evaluated, only 15 correspond to injected events, and the classifier's recall on those 15 is zero: not a single one is correctly labeled as synthetic at the current threshold. An AUROC of $0.2228$ confirms this is not just an unlucky threshold choice; the score distribution itself does not separate the two classes. In practice, this means the profit improvement from the opportunistic regime comes almost entirely from better exploitation of genuine spikes, not from avoiding synthetic ones, as the framework was designed to.

That failure also explains a second oddity in the results. We expected the $\lambda$ sensitivity curve to be an inverted U, rising as the optimizer leans harder into genuine spikes and then falling once it starts overreacting to synthetic ones. Instead, profit rises monotonically across the entire $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ range. Since no synthetic spikes are being penalized in the first place, there is nothing to pull profit back down at high $\lambda$, so the curve never turns over. A classifier that actually separates the two classes should recover that peak.

Two further limitations are more about scope than about the method itself. Everything here was run on the SP15 hub only, so we cannot yet say how well the learned VAE representations or classification thresholds would transfer to NP15, ZP26, or markets outside CAISO altogether. Furthermore, the synthetic attacks used for evaluation are non-adaptive: they are injected with fixed statistical properties and do not adapt to the detector's behavior. A more capable adversary, one with knowledge of the ensemble's decision boundary, could plausibly craft injections that mimic genuine market behavior closely enough to slip past detection~\cite{Vulnerability_Analysis}.

\section{Conclusion}

The vulnerability this paper set out to address is a practical one: BESS dispatch algorithms trust the price signals they are fed, and that trust is exploitable. Our answer is a pipeline that pairs a VAE~\cite{VAE}, iForest~\cite{IForest}, and LOF~\cite{LOF} into a three-member anomaly ensemble, then hands anything flagged to a behavioral classifier that scores it on persistence, mean reversion, and rate of change before the dispatch optimizer ever sees it. That score feeds directly into a linear program~\cite{HiGHS, Pyomo} running in one of three regimes, baseline, penalized, or opportunistic, so that confirmed genuine spikes get exploited and suspected synthetic ones get damped rather than either being ignored outright or treated with blanket suspicion.

Across three years of CAISO SP15 data, the opportunistic regime held a consistent $0.53\%$ profit edge over the anomaly-agnostic baseline under attack conditions. The classifier itself lands an $F_1$ of $0.5859$ and a precision of $0.9615$ against honest spike-mask ground truth over 727 anomalous hours, and a sweep across 15 attack severities shows detection climbing from $10.7\%$ at the mildest injections to $71.0\%$ at the most aggressive ones, with profit gains between $0.53\%$ and $1.96\%$ throughout. It also beats the two simpler baselines we compared it against: a z-score threshold actually loses money relative to baseline ($-0.98\%$), while a standalone iForest barely breaks even ($+0.27\%$). On top of the numbers, the temporal Grad-CAM~\cite{GradCAM} adaptation gives an operator a per-hour explanation for why the system charged, discharged, or sat idle, which matters in a setting where every trade eventually has to be auditable.

The clearest weakness, as discussed above, is that the classifier is not yet catching synthetic spikes specifically, so the next round of work will center on fixing that: calibrating the $is\_genuine$ threshold with the Youden-J criterion~\cite{Youden} rather than a fixed cutoff, testing whether the same pipeline holds up on NP15 and ZP26, and eventually facing it against an adversary that adapts its injections in response to what the detector is doing.
