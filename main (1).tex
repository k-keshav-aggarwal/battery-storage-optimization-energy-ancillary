\begin{abstract}
Battery energy storage systems (BESS) participating in energy markets face growing exposure to adversarial price manipulation, where artificial price spikes are injected into market signals to induce suboptimal charging and discharging decisions. Existing strategies either ignore such price events entirely, leaving the system fully exposed to manipulation, or apply blanket penalties that forfeit legitimate trading opportunities during genuine market stress. We study whether an anomaly-aware dispatch layer can preserve profit under injected price spikes without changing the underlying linear-programming battery model. The framework combines a Variational Autoencoder (VAE)~\cite{VAE} with an ensemble of Isolation Forest (iForest)~\cite{IForest} and Local Outlier Factor (LOF)~\cite{LOF} detectors to classify hourly price deviations as either genuine market events or synthetic injections. The resulting anomaly signal is integrated under three operating regimes: baseline (anomaly-agnostic), penalized (anomaly-averse), and opportunistic (anomaly-adaptive). A trained XGBoost classifier with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on twelve engineered behavioral features achieves an AUROC of $0.9723$, an $F_1$ score of $0.9938$ for the genuine class, and correctly identifies $29$ of $33$ synthetic hours passed to it by the upstream detector. The ensemble detector flags $37.5\%$ of the $88$ injected hours as anomalous at a threshold of $0.15$; the classifier then acts on this subset. Experiments on three years of California electricity price data show that the opportunistic regime achieves a $6.11\%$ profit improvement over the anomaly-agnostic baseline under adversarial conditions and a $7.70\%$ improvement under clean-market conditions. A robustness sweep across 15 spike magnitude and duration configurations confirms profit gains ranging from $4.4\%$ to $7.1\%$. Explainability of dispatch decisions is further supported by adapting Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)~\cite{GradCAM} to the temporal VAE setting, giving market operators a human-readable audit trail for every charge and discharge decision.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Battery energy storage systems (BESS) are becoming central to peer-to-peer (P2P) energy markets, where they provide price arbitrage, frequency regulation, and spinning reserve. Most of these systems now run on automated dispatch algorithms that respond directly to real-time price signals, and that dependence is exactly what makes them worth attacking. An adversary who can inject artificial price spikes into the market feed can push a battery into charging or discharging at the wrong moment, cutting into prosumer revenue and, at scale, putting real strain on grid stability~\cite{Vulnerability_Analysis}.

The objective of this paper is to test whether a detector-guided dispatch policy can distinguish genuine market stress from synthetic price injection well enough to protect revenue while keeping the underlying battery optimization tractable and auditable.

We use CAISO's SP15 hub as the primary experimental test bed, with NP15 and ZP26 retained in the data pipeline to support multi-node ingestion and future transfer checks. SP15 is one of the more actively traded parts of the CAISO market, and it already carries plenty of volatility from renewable variability and strategic bidding. Hence, an adversarial spike has to compete with substantial legitimate noise. Current dispatch strategies do not handle that noise well. One ignores anomalies altogether and treats every price tick as trustworthy, which leaves it fully exposed to manipulation. The other overcorrects: any statistically unusual price gets penalized or avoided outright, regardless of whether it came from an attacker or from a heatwave. That second strategy is safer against manipulation, but it also gives up real revenue during the exact periods, genuine supply shortfalls or demand surges, when a battery could be earning the most.

Deep generative models offer a way out of this trade-off, at least on the detection side. A VAE~\cite{VAE} trained on normal price behavior learns a compact latent representation of what ``typical'' 24-hour patterns look like, so reconstruction error becomes a natural anomaly signal when something deviates from that pattern. Classical density-based detectors such as iForest~\cite{IForest} and LOF~\cite{LOF} add a complementary view, since they are sensitive to different kinds of irregularity than a sequence model is. Even so, detection by itself does not tell you what to do about it. A spike that a detector flags as anomalous could be a real market event worth capturing, or it could be a synthetic injection worth ignoring, and a bare anomaly score has no way of distinguishing the two. That is the gap this paper tries to close: a classification layer that sits between detection and dispatch, judging each flagged spike on a trained ensemble of behavioral and temporal features before deciding whether the optimizer should lean into it or away from it.

\section{Related Work}

This work sits at the intersection of three fairly separate literatures: electricity price forecasting~\cite{Price_Forecasting}, anomaly detection in energy systems~\cite{AE_Anomaly}, and adversarial robustness in BESS dispatch~\cite{BESS}. Each has grown on its own, and none, on its own, answers the question this paper is actually interested in: whether a flagged anomaly should change what a battery does next, and by how much.

The existing approaches also fail in different ways. Threshold rules are simple but context-free, so they cannot tell a synthetic one-hour spike from a genuine heat-driven jump if both exceed the same cutoff. ARIMA-family models add temporal structure, but their linear and stationary assumptions are fragile under regime changes. LSTM and VAE-based detectors capture more temporal context, yet the literature largely stops at detection and does not connect anomaly scores to a dispatch decision. On the optimization side, MPC, stochastic programming, and reinforcement learning each improve a different part of the problem, but they usually assume the price feed is trustworthy and do not ask whether a detected anomaly should be treated as suspicious, exploitable, or both.

Statistical process control was the natural starting point for price anomaly detection: flag anything that exceeds some multiple of a rolling mean or standard deviation and call it a day. It is cheap, and for a while, that was enough. The problem is that a threshold rule has no memory of context. A demand spike during a heatwave and a five-minute synthetic injection can produce the same single-hour deviation, so a method that only looks at magnitude cannot separate them. ARIMA-family models improved on this somewhat, since they model the expected price as a function of its own history and use the residual, the gap between what was predicted and what happened, as the anomaly signal. That gives you more context than a fixed threshold, but the underlying assumption of linear, stationary dynamics does not hold up well during the regime shifts that real markets go through.

Deep learning changed the picture. LSTM-based forecasters brought reconstruction error into the anomaly-detection toolkit and turned out to be considerably better at catching multi-hour deviations than the statistical methods that came before them~\cite{LSTM_Anomaly}. VAEs~\cite{VAE} pushed this further still: because the latent space is probabilistic rather than deterministic, the model gives you a principled sense of how far an observation sits from what it considers normal, not just a raw reconstruction distance. What is missing from this line of work, though, is any attempt to close the loop. The VAE papers we are aware of stop at detection. None of them asks what a dispatch algorithm should actually do once a spike has been flagged, which is precisely the question this paper takes up.

Dispatch optimization has its own, largely separate history. Model predictive control treats dispatch as a rolling program that updates as new price forecasts come in~\cite{MPC_BESS}. Stochastic programming handles forecast uncertainty by optimizing over scenario trees, though at a real computational cost~\cite{BESS}. Reinforcement learning has also been used to learn dispatch policies directly from market interactions~\cite{RL_Dispatch, Vulnerability_Analysis}. However, several of these studies report that a well-tuned MPC baseline still beats the learned policy in volatile conditions, suggesting how hard this problem is to learn end-to-end.

What almost all of this work has in common, regardless of where it comes from, is an implicit assumption that the price feed itself can be trusted. Adversarial injection is not part of the picture, and there is no mechanism in this literature for treating an anomalous price differently depending on its source. That is the specific gap we are targeting: we extend the standard linear programming dispatch formulation with an anomaly-conditioned objective and constraint set, without giving up the tractability and auditability that make LP-based dispatch the practical choice for a regulated market in the first place.

\section{System Architecture and Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Overview}
The proposed framework processes raw electricity price data through four stages: data ingestion, anomaly detection, spike classification, and adaptive dispatch, producing a profit-maximizing charge-and-discharge schedule. In the reported experiments, the dispatch layer is evaluated on SP15, while NP15 and ZP26 remain available as additional input nodes for ingestion and transferability checks. Each stage is designed to be independent of the others: the anomaly detector can be swapped without modifying the optimizer, and the classification thresholds can be recalibrated for different market nodes without retraining the VAE. This modularity is deliberate, since operational energy systems require components that can be audited, updated, and replaced independently as market conditions or regulatory requirements change.

\subsection{Stage 1 - Data Ingestion and Preprocessing}
The pipeline ingests hourly locational marginal prices from the SP15, NP15, and ZP26 nodes of the CAISO market~\cite{CAISO}, along with four ancillary service markets: regulation up (RegUp), regulation down (RegDown), spinning reserve (Spin), and non-spinning reserve (NonSpin). To avoid repeated API calls across runs, all fetched data is stored immediately in a compressed CSV cache on first retrieval. Every subsequent run loads from this cache, which automatically restores date and time types and reduces execution time. A single aggregation step collapses the multi-node structure into a single hourly time series before passing the data downstream. Critically, synthetic spike injection is performed \emph{after} this aggregation step, ensuring that the ground-truth spike mask is exact with no dilution from the multi-node averaging.

\subsection{Stage 2 - Ensemble Anomaly Detection}
The anomaly detector is a three-member ensemble, with each member contributing a distinct perspective on whether an hourly price observation is normal or anomalous. The VAE~\cite{VAE}, the primary member, is trained on the first 80\% of the time series using 24-hour input sequences. At inference time, the hours the VAE struggles to reconstruct incur a high per-timestep reconstruction error, which forms the VAE's continuous anomaly score. The secondary members, iForest~\cite{IForest} and LOF~\cite{LOF}, are classical unsupervised outlier detectors that approach the problem from a density perspective, using continuous \texttt{score\_samples()} rather than binary classification: prices in sparsely populated regions of the distribution receive higher anomaly scores regardless of temporal context. All three detectors are trained exclusively on the training split, and the held-out test set is never used during fitting, preventing information leakage. The three continuous scores are combined into a single ensemble score in $[0, 1]$ through weighted summation, with weights of $0.5$, $0.3$, and $0.2$ respectively. These weights are fixed operating coefficients for the reported experiments; they were selected as a pragmatic balance between temporal reconstruction, density outlierness, and local-neighborhood context rather than as a claim of global optimality.

\subsection{Stage 3 - Genuine vs.\ Synthetic Spike Classification}
A high anomaly score alone is not enough to determine how the dispatch optimizer should respond: a genuine demand surge caused by a heatwave and a synthetic price injection can both produce high scores, but the correct dispatch response to each is the opposite. The classification stage resolves this ambiguity using a trained XGBoost~\cite{XGBoost} classifier fitted on twelve engineered features and evaluated with 5-fold stratified cross-validation to account for the severe class imbalance (${\approx}42$:$1$ genuine-to-synthetic ratio among anomalous hours).

The twelve input features span three categories. \emph{Behavioral signals}: (1) a persistence score measuring how many consecutive hours prices remained elevated above a rolling mean; (2) a non-reversion score comparing the current price to its forward rolling mean (genuine shocks stay elevated, synthetic injections revert sharply); and (3) a rate-of-change score capturing near-instantaneous step jumps---a signature of synthetic injection that is rare in real markets. \emph{Statistical context}: (4) rolling z-score; (5) volatility-normalized deviation. \emph{Temporal encoding}: (6,7) cyclic hour-of-day sine and cosine; (8,9) cyclic day-of-week sine and cosine. \emph{Detector scores}: (10) VAE reconstruction error; (11) iForest outlierness; (12) LOF outlierness.

An XGBoost classifier is trained with class-weighted loss (\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} set to the ratio of negative to positive samples per fold) with $n\_estimators = 200$, $max\_depth = 3$, and $learning\_rate = 0.05$. A Logistic Regression baseline with balanced class weights is also trained for comparison. The classifier outputs a continuous $is\_genuine$ probability in $[0, 1]$, which the optimizer treats as a soft weight rather than a hard binary label.

\subsection{Stage 4 - Anomaly-Adaptive Dispatch Optimization}
The dispatch optimizer is a linear program solved using the HiGHS solver~\cite{HiGHS} via Pyomo~\cite{Pyomo}. It selects an hourly charge and discharge schedule to maximize total operating profit, subject to the battery's physical constraints: SoC evolves according to round-trip efficiency, discharge is bounded by available stored energy, and charge is bounded by remaining capacity. Transaction fees and per-cycle degradation costs are included in the objective to reflect real operational economics. The three operating regimes differ only in how the anomaly signal modulates the sell constraint and objective coefficients. In the baseline regime, anomaly scores are ignored entirely. In the penalized regime, sell limits and effective price weights are reduced in proportion to the anomaly score, so the battery trades less aggressively whenever an anomaly is detected. In the opportunistic regime, the proposed method decomposes the anomaly score using the $is\_genuine$ weight: confirmed genuine spikes raise the sell limit and increase the effective sell price. In contrast, suspected synthetic spikes lower the sell limit. The degree of response in both directions is governed by the sensitivity parameter $\lambda \in [0, 1]$.

\subsection{Explainability via Temporal Grad-CAM}

To address the interpretability requirements that accompany any AI-assisted trading system operating in a regulated market, the framework adapts Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)~\cite{GradCAM} to the temporal VAE setting. In its original image-classification setting, Grad-CAM computes the gradient of the target class score with respect to the final feature map, revealing which spatial regions of the input drove the model's prediction. We apply the same idea along the time axis rather than the spatial axis: taking the gradient of the VAE's total reconstruction error with respect to each hour in the 24-hour input sequence tells us which hours the model found most anomalous and, therefore, most influential in shaping the dispatch signal.

The resulting per-hour influence scores are aligned with the original hourly time axis and stored alongside the dispatch output, giving a market operator a human-readable explanation of why the system chose to charge, discharge, or remain idle at any given hour. \section{Model Equations and Parameters}

The battery SoC is updated according to

\begin{equation}
SOC_t = SOC_{t-1} + \left( buy_t \cdot e - \frac{sell_t}{e} \right) dt
\end{equation}

where charging and discharging are constrained by

\begin{equation}
sell_t \leq e \cdot SOC_{t-1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
buy_t \cdot e \leq Capacity_{\mathrm{MWh}} - SOC_{t-1}
\end{equation}

The maximum discharge power is limited by

\begin{equation}
sell_t \leq P_{\max}
\end{equation}

The objective is to maximize net revenue:

\begin{equation}
\max \sum_t \Bigl( sell_t \cdot price_t - buy_t \cdot price_t - cost \cdot (sell_t + buy_t) \Bigr)
\end{equation}

\section{Dataset Description}

\subsection{Data Sources}
The dataset was obtained from the California Independent System Operator (CAISO) wholesale electricity market~\cite{CAISO} via the \texttt{gridstatus} API. Three major trading hubs were selected to represent geographically diverse regions of the California power system:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SP15} (South Path 15 Trading Hub)
    \item \textbf{NP15} (North Path 15 Trading Hub)
    \item \textbf{ZP26} (Valley Electric Trading Hub)
\end{itemize}

Ancillary service market prices were also retrieved to provide operational signals: Regulation Up (RegUp), Regulation Down (RegDown), Spinning Reserve (Spin), and Non-Spinning Reserve (NonSpin).

\subsection{Data Collection Procedure}
Historical market data spanning January 2023 through December 2025 were obtained through the CAISO day-ahead market interface. To improve reliability and avoid API timeouts, the data acquisition process used chunk-based retrieval, dividing the full time range into 15-day intervals. Failed requests were automatically retried before being discarded. Retrieved datasets were cached locally to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Dataset Characteristics}
The final dataset consists of 77,832 hourly market observations across three trading hubs. Each record contains the Locational Marginal Price (LMP) value and ancillary service prices aligned to a common timestamp.

\subsection{Synthetic Anomaly Generation}
To test detection performance under adversarial conditions, we built a synthetic attack dataset by injecting controlled price spikes, using a local random number generator with a fixed seed to ensure reproducibility. Spike injection is performed on the aggregated hourly time series (one row per hour) rather than the raw multi-node dataframe, ensuring that the ground-truth spike mask is exact with no dilution from node averaging. For each injected event, we picked a random temporal window and scaled the original LMP signal within it by a configurable magnitude and duration. Ninety percent of injected events were non-reverting spikes, meant to simulate sustained adversarial manipulation rather than a one-off blip. We also kept a Boolean spike mask indicating which hours were injected so that the classifier could be evaluated against honest ground truth. The synthetic label set is intentionally small relative to the total number of anomalous hours, so the classification metrics should be interpreted as evidence from a sparse injection study and cross-validated results should be preferred over single-split estimates.

Two datasets were maintained throughout all experiments:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clean Dataset}: Original CAISO market data.
    \item \textbf{Attack Dataset}: Market data with 16 synthetic price spike events (88 injected hours).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Train/Test Partitioning}
We partitioned the dataset chronologically rather than randomly to preserve temporal dependencies and avoid leaking future information into the training set. All three detectors, VAE, iForest, and LOF, were fit exclusively on the training split (80\%), and the held-out test set was used only for final evaluation.

\subsection{Dataset Challenges}
Several characteristics of electricity market data present challenges for anomaly detection:

\begin{itemize}
    \item High price volatility and rare extreme spikes.
    \item Severe class imbalance between normal and anomalous events.
    \item Regional market differences between trading hubs.
    \item Seasonal and operational regime changes over multi-year spans.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
VAE reconstruction performance is assessed using MAE, RMSE, $R^2$, final validation loss, and reconstruction accuracy. Classifier performance is assessed using precision, recall, $F_1$, and AUROC for both the genuine and the synthetic class; 5-fold stratified cross-validation out-of-fold predictions are used for all classifier metrics to avoid overfitting on the small positive class. Dispatch performance is reported as total profit (\$K), equivalent full cycles (EFC), and profit per cycle (\$/MWh). A consistent anomaly score threshold of $0.15$ is used throughout all experiments, including the classifier evaluation and the robustness sweep.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{VAE Reconstruction Performance}
The VAE was trained on the first 80\% of each scenario's time series and evaluated on the remaining 20\%. The VAE is not designed as a conventional price forecaster; it is trained as a density model that learns the distribution of normal 24-hour price sequences. Under this framing, $R^2$ is not the primary evaluation metric here: a model that achieves high $R^2$ by tracking prices closely, including anomalous ones, would defeat the purpose of anomaly detection. The clean scenario yields an $R^2$ of $0.0625$ and the attack scenario $0.1251$, both near zero and consistent with a model that generalizes to typical patterns without overfitting to extremes. Reconstruction accuracy improves from $21.57\%$ under clean conditions to $24.17\%$ under attack, a $12.06\%$ relative increase, confirming that the VAE's reconstruction error grows in the presence of injected anomalies, precisely the signal the downstream stages rely on. \subsection{Anomaly Detection and Classification Performance}
The anomaly detection pipeline blends per-timestep VAE reconstruction error, continuous iForest outlierness scores, and continuous LOF scores into a unified ensemble anomaly score with weights $0.5$, $0.3$, and $0.2$, respectively. A consistent anomaly threshold of $0.15$ is applied throughout all experiments.

\textbf{Detection-stage recall.} Of the 88 injected synthetic hours, $33$ are flagged as anomalous by the ensemble detector at the $0.15$ threshold, yielding a detection-stage recall of $37.5\%$. The remaining $55$ synthetic hours are not passed to the classifier at all; this upstream detection gap is an inherent limitation of the ensemble at this spike magnitude and is reported explicitly rather than absorbed into the classifier metrics.

\textbf{Genuine-vs-synthetic classification.} The XGBoost classifier is evaluated on 1,416 anomalous hours using 5-fold stratified cross-validation. Of these, 33 correspond to injected synthetic spikes and 1,383 are genuine market events. XGBoost achieves an AUROC of $0.9723$ and an average precision of $0.9992$, substantially outperforming the Logistic Regression baseline (AUROC $0.9258$). All classifier metrics are based on out-of-fold predictions from 5-fold stratified cross-validation, not a single train/test split, and should be interpreted with the caveat that the positive class contains only 33 examples. \subsection{BESS Dispatch Optimization Results}
BESS dispatch is solved as a linear program using HiGHS~\cite{HiGHS} via Pyomo~\cite{Pyomo}. Three strategies are evaluated: Baseline, Penalized, and Opportunistic. Under the standard evaluation with $\lambda = 0.3$, the opportunistic regime achieves a profit of \$522.5\,K under clean conditions and \$528.0\,K under attack, compared to the baseline's \$485.1\,K and \$497.6\,K, representing a $7.70\%$ improvement under clean conditions and $6.11\%$ under attack. Strategy profits and EFC statistics are summarized in Tables~\ref{tab:strategy_results} and~\ref{tab:efc_profit_cycle}.

A sensitivity analysis over $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ was conducted across both clean and attack scenarios. The penalized strategy converges to a stable lower bound as $\lambda$ approaches $1.0$, reflecting its increasing reluctance to trade during anomalous periods. \subsection{Comparison Against SOTA Methods}
The proposed VAE ensemble was compared against two simpler anomaly detectors within the same opportunistic dispatch pipeline: a rolling z-score threshold and a standalone iForest using continuous outlierness scores. Under attack conditions, the z-score threshold achieves a profit change of $-0.98\%$ over baseline, the standalone iForest achieves $+0.27\%$, and the proposed VAE ensemble with the trained XGBoost classifier achieves $+6.11\%$. The VAE ensemble is the only detector that consistently improves over the baseline in both clean and attack scenarios. The z-score threshold underperforms because it flags legitimate volatility periods as anomalous, causing the optimizer to suppress trades during genuine price events. The iForest alone captures point-level outliers but lacks the temporal coherence of the VAE~\cite{VAE}, leading to noisier anomaly signals. The VAE ensemble's advantage comes from combining temporal structure (VAE), density-level sensitivity (iForest~\cite{IForest}), and local neighborhood context (LOF~\cite{LOF}) into a more reliable anomaly signal for the downstream XGBoost classifier.

\section{Limitations}

The profit gains reported above are real and substantial, but they come with limitations worth being upfront about.

\textbf{Detection-stage recall.} The most important limitation is not in the classifier but in the upstream detector. Of the 88 injected synthetic hours, only 33 are flagged as anomalous by the ensemble at the $0.15$ threshold, a detection-stage recall of $37.5\%$. The classifier can only act on the hours the detector passes it. The $55$ undetected hours are processed by the optimizer without any anomaly signal, meaning the framework offers no protection for nearly two-thirds of injected events at this spike magnitude. Raising the threshold would increase detection recall but also increase false positives on genuine hours, so the threshold choice is a real operating trade-off.

\textbf{Small positive-class size.} With only 33 synthetic hours available to the classifier after detection, cross-validated metrics are more trustworthy than a single holdout split, but the sample is still small. The XGBoost metrics should be read as indicative of the classifier's behavior on this specific injection configuration rather than as definitive performance bounds; a larger injection study would be needed to characterize generalization.

\textbf{Transfer risk.} Everything here was run on the SP15 hub only, so we cannot yet say how well the learned VAE representations, classification thresholds, or XGBoost feature importances transfer to NP15, ZP26, or markets outside CAISO altogether.

\textbf{Non-adaptive adversary.} The synthetic attacks used for evaluation are non-adaptive: they are injected with fixed statistical properties and do not adapt to the detector's behavior. A more capable adversary, one with knowledge of the ensemble's decision boundary, could plausibly craft injections that mimic genuine market behavior closely enough to slip past detection~\cite{Vulnerability_Analysis}.

\textbf{Monotonic $\lambda$-curve.} The opportunistic profit curve rises monotonically with $\lambda$ across $[0, 1]$. This is a legitimate and explainable finding given that genuine anomalous hours vastly outnumber detected synthetic ones, and it is reported as such rather than as an expected inverted-U shape. An inverted U would require a scenario where a significant fraction of detected anomalous hours are synthetic; at the current injection density that scenario does not arise.

\section{Conclusion}

The vulnerability this paper set out to address is a practical one: BESS dispatch algorithms trust the price signals they are fed, and that trust is exploitable. Our answer is a pipeline that pairs a VAE~\cite{VAE}, iForest~\cite{IForest}, and LOF~\cite{LOF} into a three-member anomaly ensemble, then hands anything flagged to a trained XGBoost~\cite{XGBoost} classifier evaluated with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on twelve behavioral and temporal features. That classifier outputs a continuous $is\_genuine$ probability that feeds directly into a linear program~\cite{HiGHS, Pyomo} running in one of three regimes---baseline, penalized, or opportunistic---so that confirmed genuine spikes get exploited and suspected synthetic ones get damped rather than either being ignored outright or treated with blanket suspicion.

Across three years of CAISO SP15 data, the opportunistic regime achieved a $6.11\%$ profit edge over the anomaly-agnostic baseline under attack conditions and a $7.70\%$ edge under clean-market conditions. The XGBoost classifier achieves an AUROC of $0.9723$ and an $F_1$ of $0.9938$ for the genuine class, with $29$ of $33$ detected synthetic hours correctly identified, all assessed via out-of-fold cross-validation. A sweep across 15 attack severities shows opportunistic dispatch profit gains between $4.4\%$ and $7.1\%$, confirming that the framework is robust across spike magnitudes and durations. It also beats the two simpler baselines: a z-score threshold loses money relative to baseline ($-0.98\%$), while a standalone iForest barely breaks even ($+0.27\%$). On top of the numbers, the temporal Grad-CAM~\cite{GradCAM} adaptation gives an operator a per-hour explanation for why the system charged, discharged, or sat idle, which matters in a setting where every trade eventually has to be auditable.

The clearest areas for future work follow from the detection-stage gap: only $37.5\%$ of injected hours are flagged by the ensemble at the $0.15$ threshold, so the next round of work should focus on improving ensemble sensitivity at lower spike magnitudes, testing whether the same pipeline holds up on NP15 and ZP26, expanding the injection study to generate a larger positive class for more reliable classifier evaluation, and eventually facing it against an adversary that adapts its injections in response to what the detector is doing.
